Chapter 1

Four circles that touch

Take three circles, each touching the other two. There is room for a fourth that touches all three — and the four sizes cannot then be anything you like. One equation ties them together.

The relation was found by Descartes in 1643, in a letter to Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, and lost again. It has been rediscovered several times since. The version most worth quoting is not an equation at all.

The Kiss Precise

Frederick Soddy won a Nobel Prize for chemistry — he named the isotope — and in 1936 published the theorem in Nature as a poem.

For pairs of lips to kiss maybe
Involves no trigonometry.
’Tis not so when four circles kiss
Each one the other three.
To bring this off the four must be
As three in one or one in three.
If one in three, beyond a doubt
Each gets three kisses from without.
If three in one, then is that one
Thrice kissed internally.

Four circles to the kissing come.
The smaller are the benter.
The bend is just the inverse of
The distance from the centre.
Though their intrigue left Euclid dumb
There’s now no need for rule of thumb.
Since zero bend’s a dead straight line
And concave bends have minus sign,
The sum of the squares of all four bends
Is half the square of their sum.

Frederick Soddy, “The Kiss Precise”, Nature 137, 1021 (20 June 1936). A third stanza extends the result to spheres.

The last two lines are the theorem. Writing b for a bend:

b₁² + b₂² + b₃² + b₄²  =  ½ (b₁ + b₂ + b₃ + b₄)²

Everything this project does rests on that line. The figure below works it out for whichever quadruple you choose — the numbers are computed as you look, in exact integer arithmetic, not printed from a table.

What Soddy is telling you in the second stanza

Three lines of that verse are the conventions this program is built on, and they are easy to skim past.

  • “The smaller are the benter.” A bend is 1/r. Tiny circles have enormous bends, which is why the deep parts of a packing carry huge numbers.
  • “Zero bend’s a dead straight line.” A line is a circle of infinite radius, so its bend is 0. Choose the strip in the figure: two of the four are lines, and the identity holds unchanged.
  • “Concave bends have minus sign.” When one circle encloses the other three, its bend counts as negative. That is the −1 in (−1, 2, 2, 3), and without it the arithmetic fails.

Soddy also states the two configurations: “as three in one or one in three”. Either three circles sit inside a fourth, or four sit outside one another. Both appear in the figure.

Where this is going

The equation is quadratic in each bend, and that is the whole story of the next chapter. Fix three circles and ask for the fourth: a quadratic has two roots, so there are two circles touching all three — a small one in the gap between them, and a large one wrapped around the outside.

Which raises the question the rest of this depends on. If both answers are legitimate, and each of them makes a new triple with two answers of its own, how much of the plane can you fill?
