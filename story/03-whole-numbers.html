Chapter 3

Why the numbers stay whole

Every bend in the packing is an integer, at every depth, forever. So is every centre a ratio of them. The picture never needs a real number at all — and the reason it does not is a constraint with a price.

The picture never leaves ℚ(i)

A circle here is three numbers: (b̄, b, b·z), with the bend b an ordinary integer and b·z a Gaussian integer — a number m + ni with m, n whole. The centre is then b·z divided by b, and the radius is 1/b.

Which means every centre in every packing this program draws is a ratio of Gaussian integers, with denominator exactly the bend, and every radius is the reciprocal of a whole number. The whole picture lives in ℚ(i) — the Gaussian rationals, the plane's version of ordinary fractions. Not the reals. Not even the algebraic numbers. Fractions.

That is not a convenience of this implementation, it is what the object is. And it survives the recursion because chapter 2's rule is b′ = 2(b₁ + b₂ + b₃) − b: addition, doubling, subtraction. No division, no square root. Integers are closed under those, so you cannot leave them however deep you go.

The four bends force everything else

Three mutually tangent circles with given radii are rigid: the triangle joining their centres has sides r₁+r₂, r₂+r₃, r₃+r₁, and a triangle with given sides has one shape. Drop in the fourth by chapter 2's jump and it is determined too.

So four bends satisfying Descartes do not merely constrain the picture — they are the picture, up to sliding and turning it. There is exactly one freedom left, and it is a coin flip: the mirror image.

Eight orientations, or four

The mirror image is rational too. Reflecting the plane conjugates every coordinate, m + ni ↦ m − ni, which maps the Gaussian integers onto themselves. You cannot fall out of ℤ[i] by looking at a packing in a mirror.

So count how many ways a given set of bends can actually sit on the lattice. The square lattice has eight symmetries — four rotations, and each of those mirrored — and every one of them sends a rational configuration to a rational configuration. That gives up to eight orientations, four per handedness:

All bends distinct — (−2, 3, 6, 7), (−6, 11, 14, 15), (−3, 4, 12, 13) — eight orientations. The mirror is a genuinely different configuration, so there is a left-handed set of four and a right-handed set of four.
Two bends equal — (−1, 2, 2, 3), (−3, 5, 8, 8), (−4, 8, 9, 9) — four. The repeated pair gives the configuration a mirror of its own, so the two handednesses coincide and there are only two per side.

Quotient the turning away and what is left is just the handedness: two configurations, or one. Checked against the first twelve root quadruples, without exception. There is no third case, and nothing forces you out of the rationals at any point.

And nothing ever needs reducing

A rational number can always be put in lowest terms. A quadruple can too — divide the four bends by their common factor — and the striking thing is that you never have to.

Every quadruple the recursion produces already has gcd = 1. Checked over 13,961 quadruples inside the classic packing and 65 more reached by reflecting outward: not one has a common factor. Never 2, never 3, never anything.

Which is why (−3, 5, 8, 8) and not (−3, 6, 6, 9) is what appears when the classic packing is turned inside out through its 3. The second is a perfectly valid quadruple — 3 × (−1, 2, 2, 3) — but it has a common factor, and the recursion never lands on it.

Where the picture sits, exactly

One more thing is rational, and it is the one that closes the argument. So far the coordinates have been rational relative to some placement. But the placement itself is pinned down, because the recursion has a landmark: the strip.

Turn any packing inside out repeatedly — the outward lab does this — and you arrive at (0, 0, 1, 1), two parallel lines with circles between them. That configuration hands you a coordinate frame with nothing left to choose: one axis is its line of symmetry, the other is its first straight line, and the circle of bend 1 fixes the unit, its diameter being 2.

So every circle in every one of these packings has an exactly known rational position — not merely rational up to an arbitrary choice of origin and scale, but rational in a frame the construction itself determines. There is no step anywhere, from the strip to any depth in any direction, at which a real number is required.

And nothing is secretly repeated. Of the 2,075 distinct bend signatures in the classic packing out to bend 2,000, every one occurs four times, twice or once — and never more. Four is the order of the packing's symmetry group, so each count is exactly an orbit: four for a quadruple with no symmetry of its own, two when a mirror fixes it, one for the root that everything fixes. No signature turns up by coincidence in two unrelated places.

Why symmetry is the thing you cannot have

It is natural to want more symmetry than that. Suppose the three positive bends were all equal — a bounding circle with three identical circles inside, turning into itself under a third of a turn. Ask Descartes what that costs:

(3b − a)² = 2(a² + 3b²)  ⟹  3b² − 6ab − a² = 0  ⟹  b = a(1 ± 2/√3)

Irrational, for every integer a. The three-fold symmetric configuration exists perfectly well as a picture — it just has no integral version, and no rational one. The same happens, worse, for four-fold.

So an integral packing gets a mirror or two and nothing more: the classic (−1, 2, 2, 3) has the symmetry of a rectangle, order 4, and that is the ceiling. The numbers stay whole because the picture gives up its symmetry. Those are the same fact, seen from two sides.

So how long would floating point last?

Longer than you would guess — which is worth knowing, because it means the case for exact arithmetic is not the one usually made for it. Below, the same chain computed twice: once in exact integers, once in ordinary double precision. Both do nothing but add, double and subtract.

The two columns agree perfectly for 33 steps, all the way to a bend of 7,464,241,803,632,015. Doubles carry 53 bits of mantissa, so every integer up to 2⁵³ ≈ 9.007 × 10¹⁵ is represented exactly — and until the bends pass that line, floating point is doing integer arithmetic and getting integer answers.

And then it fails quietly

At step 34 the true bend is 21,572,059,181,468,678. That is past 2⁵³, so a double cannot hold it. What it holds instead is 21,572,059,181,468,680 — wrong by 2.

Notice what does not happen. There is no error, no exception, no ragged decimal. The wrong answer is still a whole number, still plausible, still eleven digits of correct prefix. Nothing about it looks broken.

It is worse than that. Ask a floating-point program to check its answer against the true value and it will report agreement — because converting the true value to a double gives the same wrong number. The error is not merely unnoticed; from inside floating point it is not measurable. You cannot find this bug by testing in the arithmetic that has it.

What exactness actually buys

Not "floats break immediately" — they plainly do not. The gain is that rounding never compounds. In a floating-point recursion every step inherits the last step's error and adds its own, so the damage grows with depth. Here the arithmetic is exact all the way down, and a number is rounded exactly once, at the last moment, when a circle is handed to the canvas to be drawn.

That is why src/math/ is BigInt throughout and why nothing in it imports anything that draws. One rounding per circle, at the edge of the system, never fed back in.

So the picture is made of integers — exactly, at any depth, and not merely nearly. Which raises the question the next two chapters answer: how many are there, and which integers?
