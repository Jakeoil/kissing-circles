Chapter 4

Generations

The jump makes one new circle from three old ones. Do it everywhere there is a gap and the packing arrives in rounds — and the rounds can be counted exactly.

Start with four mutually tangent circles. Any three of them bound a curvilinear triangle, and chapter 2's jump drops a circle into it. There are four such triples — 4 choose 3 — so the first round adds four circles.

Now count the second round. Each of those four new circles arrived in a gap bounded by three older circles, and it has divided that gap into three smaller ones. So each new circle opens three new gaps, and each gap takes exactly one circle:

4 · 3ⁿ⁻¹

Four circles in round 1, tripling every round after. Round n adds 4·3ⁿ⁻¹ circles: 4, 12, 36, 108, 324, 972, 2916 — checked against the generator through round 8, where it predicts 8,748 and gets 8,748.

Add up the rounds and the running total collapses to something tidier than the sum suggests. Counting the original four:

4 + 4 + 12 + 36 + ⋯ + 4·3ⁿ⁻¹  =  2·3ⁿ + 2

So 8 circles after one round, 20 after two, 56 after three, 164 after four. The packing grows by a factor of three forever, which is why the workbench talks about depth rather than about totals — round 20 alone would be about four thousand million circles, and no screen has that many pixels.

What the rounds are not

A round is not a size. It is tempting to read generation as "how small", and mostly it tracks: curvature grows down every branch, so later rounds are smaller on average. But the bend of a new circle is 2(b₁+b₂+b₃) − b₄, and how big that is depends on which gap it lands in, not on how deep the recursion has gone. In the figure above, some round-4 circles are visibly larger than some round-3 ones.

And the colours are not the same colours as elsewhere. This figure paints by round, because rounds are its subject. Chapters 1, 2 and 5 paint by bend modulo 24, which is a fact about arithmetic rather than about the recursion. Switch color above and watch the two 6s change from different colours to the same one: one arrives in round 1 and the other in round 2, but they are the same number and the same class. Neither view is more correct — they answer different questions.

And a round is not a slice through the picture. Peeling back with the control above hides circles by their generation, which is a fact about *how they were derived*, not about where they sit. Two circles that touch can be six rounds apart.

An aside that cost me a crash. Building the strip packing (0, 0, 1, 1) with a bound on curvature and none on area exhausts memory, and it is not a bug. The strip repeats left and right forever, so translating any circle by one unit gives another circle of the same bend. There are infinitely many circles of curvature 4,000 in it. For a periodic packing, "small enough" is not a finite condition — only "small enough and near enough" is.

Every one of these bends is a whole number, in every round, forever — chapter 3 is about why that survives. The question this chapter ends on is the other one: which whole numbers? Not all of them turn up, and what is missing is stranger than what is present.
