Chapter 5

Which numbers appear

Every bend in the packing is a whole number. Not every whole number is a bend — and the ones that go missing are the most interesting thing in the picture.

Chapter 4 counted how many circles arrive in each round. This one asks what is written on them. Take the classic packing, list every bend it produces, and sort the integers by their remainder on division by 24.

The two halves of that figure are the same colours. Above, the packing, with each circle painted by its bend's remainder. Below, the grid of integers. A column's colour is the colour of the circles in that class — find the yellow-green column and every yellow-green circle in the packing is one of its members. The grid is the packing's arithmetic with the geometry taken away.

Most of the columns are empty

Two thirds of the grid is dark, and not because the packing ran out of room. Sixteen of the twenty-four remainders never occur at all in the classic packing — at any size, in any part of it. It uses only 2, 3, 6, 11, 14, 15, 18, 23.

This is a congruence condition, and it comes straight out of Descartes' theorem. The relation ties the four bends together tightly enough that once the root quadruple is fixed, the whole packing is confined to a handful of classes modulo 24. Which classes, and how many, depend on the root — step the selector through and watch the columns move:

(−1, 2, 2, 3)  →  2 3 6 11 14 15 18 23
(−6, 11, 14, 15)  →  2 3 6 11 14 15 18 23 — the very same eight
(−2, 3, 6, 7)  →  3 6 7 10 15 18 19 22 — a different eight
(−3, 5, 8, 8)  →  0 5 8 12 20 21 — only six

So two different packings can occupy exactly the same classes, and the count is not always eight. What is fixed is the modulus.

Where 24 comes from

24 = 8 × 3, and the two halves come from different places. The bends of the classic packing satisfy two independent conditions — b ≡ 2, 3, 6, 7 (mod 8) and b ≡ 0, 2 (mod 3) — and four choices times two choices is the eight columns in the figure. The Chinese remainder theorem glues them into a single statement modulo 24.

The mod 3 half falls straight out of the theorem. Write S = b₁+b₂+b₃+b₄ and Q = b₁²+b₂²+b₃²+b₄², so Descartes says S² = 2Q. Squares modulo 3 are only 0 or 1, so if k is how many of the four bends are not divisible by 3, then Q ≡ k. Checking the two cases — S ≡ 0 forces k ≡ 0, otherwise k ≡ 2 — and knowing a primitive packing cannot have all four bends divisible by 3, the only survivors are k = 3 and k = 2. Every Descartes quadruple therefore contains at least one bend divisible by 3. The mod 8 half works the same way, from the fact that squares modulo 8 are only 0, 1 and 4.

And 24 is where it stops. Generating the classic packing to 30,000 gives 9,947 distinct bends; count how many classes they occupy at each modulus and the answer is 2 of 3, 4 of 8, 8 of 24 — then 16 of 48, 24 of 72, 80 of 240. Past 24 every count is exactly what you get if the classes simply split evenly, with no new condition. Nothing finer than 24 knows anything 24 does not.

And then there are the holes

These are not an artefact of stopping early. Bends grow down every branch, so generating until every branch has passed 10,000 finds every bend at or below 10,000 that the packing will ever contain. The gaps are real.

The conjecture that was wrong

For a long time the expectation — the local–global conjecture — was that the holes run out. Congruence conditions would rule out most integers, and every sufficiently large integer that survived them would eventually appear as a bend. The exceptions would be a finite nuisance, a short list near the bottom.

In 2023 that turned out to be false. Summer Haag, Clyde Kertzer, James Rickards and Katherine Stange showed there are further obstructions beyond the congruence ones — coming from quadratic and quartic reciprocity — and that for some packings infinitely many admissible integers never occur.

So the list above is not a table of exceptions waiting to be explained away. It is the visible end of something that keeps going. This is a picture you can draw in a browser with an open problem inside it.
