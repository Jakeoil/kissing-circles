Chapter 6

Two kinds of region

The Apollonian move — drop a circle into the gap between three that touch — is one of two rules. Here is the other, and the reason the two must be drawn apart before they are drawn together.

Asmus Schmidt's 1975 algorithm subdivides the complex plane the way continued fractions subdivide the line. It works on regions, and there are exactly two shapes of region. Which shape you have decides how many pieces you get.

A circular region — a disc, or a half plane, which is a disc through infinity — splits into seven. A triangular region — the curvilinear triangle between three mutually tangent circles — splits into four. Schmidt drew these as two separate figures, and it matters that he did.

The circular rule

Start with the upper half plane, 𝒥. Its seven children are three circular — 𝒱₁ 𝒱₂ 𝒱₃ — and four triangular — ℰ₁ ℰ₂ ℰ₃ and C. The 𝒱 pieces sit at the three corners, the ℰ pieces along the three edges, and C in the middle. That is what the letters mean: vertex, edge, centre.

In coordinates the first generation is the unit square. The lines y = 0, y = 1, x = 0 and x = 1, with a circle of radius ½ at the midpoint of each side. Look at it and you may see two strip packings laid over one another, one horizontal and one vertical — which is exactly what it is. The curvatures are 2 rather than 1, because Schmidt's normalization makes every curvature in the arrangement even.

The triangular rule

Now take one of those curvilinear triangles. It splits into four: three more triangles at its corners, 𝒱₁* 𝒱₂* 𝒱₃*, and a single circle C* dropped into the middle.

That last part is the Apollonian move. Three mutually tangent circles, and one more inscribed in the gap between them — the circle you get from Descartes' theorem, the one this project has been generating all along. In Schmidt's scheme it is a single letter, c, applied to a triangular region.

So an Apollonian packing is what you get by using only the C rule and ignoring the 𝒱 and ℰ pieces entirely. The gasket is not a different object from the arrangement. It is a sub-orbit — one letter's worth of a seven-letter alphabet.

Why they have to be drawn apart

Press both at once above. The result is a mess, and instructively so: the two subdivisions are of different regions, so superimposing their boundaries produces curves that never bound anything together. It looks like structure and is not.

This is the failure mode of drawing an arrangement as circles rather than as regions. Every published picture of a Schmidt arrangement is a tangle of circles precisely because the region structure has been thrown away — and the first version of the lab page in this project made the same mistake, which is how this chapter came to be written.
