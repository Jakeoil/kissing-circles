Chapter 8

Ford circles, one dimension down

Everything so far has happened over the Gaussian integers, in the plane. Run the same idea over the ordinary integers, on a line, and something familiar falls out — the continued fraction.

Put a circle on every rational number. At p/q in lowest terms, sit a circle tangent to the real line at that point, with radius 1/(2q²) — so the bigger the denominator, the smaller the circle. These are Ford circles, and their bend is 2q².

They never overlap, and they touch for a reason

No two Ford circles ever cross. Any two are either disjoint or exactly tangent, and there is a clean rule for which:

p/q and r/s are tangent  ⟺  |ps − qr| = 1

That is the condition for two fractions to be Farey neighbours — adjacent at some stage of the Farey sequence. So the tangency graph of the Ford circles is the neighbour relation on the rationals. Geometry and arithmetic are the same statement here, which is the whole reason the picture is worth drawing.

Mediants: the subdivision

Between two neighbours p/q and r/s, the fraction that goes in the gap is the mediant:

p/q ⊕ r/s = (p + r)/(q + s)

Add the tops, add the bottoms — the thing every schoolchild is told is wrong, and which is exactly right here. Its Ford circle drops into the curvilinear gap between the other two and touches both. That is the Apollonian move, one dimension down. Press + above and watch each new round of circles land in the gaps left by the last, exactly as chapter 4's circles did in the plane.

The mediant is Descartes' theorem

That last claim can be made exact, and it is the best thing on this page. Take the real line — a circle of bend 0 — together with the Ford circles of two Farey neighbours, bends q² and s². Those three are mutually tangent, so chapter 1 applies: there are exactly two circles completing them, and chapter 2 says their bends are

q² + s² ± 2√(q²s²)  =  q² + s² ± 2qs  =  (q + s)²  or  (q − s)²

The two completions are the mediant and its reverse. Not analogous to them — they are them. (q + s)² is the Ford circle of the mediant (p+r)/(q+s), dropped into the gap; (q − s)² is the one on the other side, the neighbour you came from. Descartes' theorem, applied to a line and two Ford circles, says add the denominators.

So (0, q², s², (q+s)²) is a Descartes quadruple for every pair of Farey neighbours — (0, 1, 1, 4), (0, 1, 4, 9), (0, 4, 9, 25), (0, 1, 9, 16) — and the square numbers appearing as bends is exactly the 1/(2q²) radius the chapter opened with, halved out of the arrangement's convention.

These turned up sideways. Reflecting the classic packing's quadruples outward (the outward lab, plan.md §8.6) produces degenerate configurations with one straight line, and their bends came out 1, 4, 9, 16, 25. Squares, because they are Ford quadruples — the outward move lands on this chapter as well as on new packings. Nobody was looking for that.

Do this from 0/1 and 1/1 and you generate every rational in [0, 1], each exactly once, in lowest terms and with no repeats. The tree of mediants is the Stern–Brocot tree, and a path down it is a continued fraction: go left, left, right, left … and the fraction you converge on is the number whose expansion is that sequence.

These circles are in the arrangement. Chapter 7's Schmidt arrangement of ℤ[i] contains them — a Ford circle at p/q is the row (2p², 2q², 2pq + i), which satisfies the same invariant |bz|² − b·b̄ = 1 as everything else in this project. Of those with q ≤ 20, generation 8 of the arrangement already contains 85; the rest lie deeper than it reaches. Not deeper than they exist — deeper than the generator currently goes, which is a limitation of the pruning noted in plan.md §0 and not a fact about the mathematics.

What this was rehearsal for

On the line, a real number is pinned down by a nested sequence of intervals, each chosen from the two on either side of a mediant. That sequence of choices is its continued fraction, and the Ford circles make it visible: the number sits under a shrinking chain of tangent circles.

Now put the plane back. Schmidt's regions from chapter 6 are the interval's replacement, the subdivision from chapter 7 is the mediant's, and a complex number is pinned down by a nested sequence of regions rather than intervals. So what does a continued fraction look like on the plane? That is the last chapter, and it is the question this whole project started from.
