Chapter 9

Walking to ei

A real number's continued fraction is the sequence of intervals closing in on it. A complex number's is the sequence of regions. Here is one being computed, and it comes out matching a formula written down in 1975.

Chapter 8 left the question. On the line, you pin a number down by choosing, at each mediant, which side it falls on. In the plane, chapter 6 gave you seven pieces to choose from instead of two, and chapter 7 applied that everywhere. So the algorithm writes itself: ask which child region contains the number, step into it, and repeat.

The letters that come out — 𝒱₁ 𝒱₂ 𝒱₃ ℰ₁ ℰ₂ ℰ₃ C — are the expansion. Nothing else is needed. There is no division, no remainder, no ⌊x⌋: just containment, tested against the exact circles from chapter 6.

What the walk says about ei

Press walk with e^i selected and let it run. The word is not random and not periodic. It falls into blocks:

C C* 𝒱₃ · C C* 𝒱₃³ · C C* 𝒱₃⁵ · C C* 𝒱₃⁷ · C C* 𝒱₃⁹ · C C* 𝒱₃¹¹ · …

A pair of C moves, then a run of 𝒱₃; and the runs go 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11 — the odd numbers, in order. Schmidt derived that closed form in 1975, and this program does not know it. The walk above tests geometric containment and nothing else; the pattern is what comes out.

That is the check worth having. A picture can be made to look convincing by accident. A word that reproduces a formula derived independently, fifty years earlier, cannot.

If this feels familiar, it should. The ordinary continued fraction of e is [2; 1, 2, 1, 1, 4, 1, 1, 6, 1, 1, 8, …] — a fixed preamble and then even numbers climbing by two. One dimension up, ei gives a fixed preamble and odd numbers climbing by two. The same number keeping the same kind of secret in a different alphabet.

Where it stops, and why that is the right ending

The walk halts around depth 50. Not because the subdivision runs out — it is exact and goes on forever — but because ei arrives here as a double. By depth 50 the regions are smaller than the last bit of a 64-bit float, and the number can no longer say which one it is in.

Chapter 3 made the case for exact arithmetic and drew the line at the edge of the system: one rounding per circle, at draw time, never fed back. This is the other edge. The machinery is exact; the input is not. Feed it a Gaussian rational and it would run as deep as you like — which is exactly why the interesting numbers are the ones that are not.

Three kinds of number

Try the other presets, and the parallel with ordinary continued fractions comes out complete:

  • A rational point terminates. (1+i)/2 is a vertex of the subdivision, so the walk reaches it and stops — as 3/8 has a finite continued fraction and nothing after it.
  • Some numbers go periodic. φ·i repeats 𝒱₁ 𝒱₂ forever, the way the golden ratio is [1; 1, 1, 1, …]. i settles into 𝒱₂* repeating after a short preamble — eventually periodic, like a quadratic irrational.
  • And some do neither, but are not thereby random. That is ei.

The figure that was wanted

In March 2004, writing about Gaussian integers and complex continued fractions, Ed Pegg Jr described this construction and said he could not produce the picture. That column is what set this project going, and this chapter is that picture: the region containing ei, generation by generation, in exact integer arithmetic, in a browser.

Which is the end of the story, and about a fifth of what is in the paper. Schmidt's construction runs over ℤ[i]; there are other imaginary quadratic fields, each with its own arrangement and its own continued fraction. The workbench is still there, and it still goes deeper than you can look.

Asmus L. Schmidt, “Diophantine approximation of complex numbers”, Acta Mathematica 134 (1975), 1–85 — the algorithm, and the closed form for the expansion. Ed Pegg Jr, “Gaussian Numbers”, MAA Math Games, 15 March 2004.
